Best PoE+ HAT for Raspberry Pi 5 to Eliminate Power Adapters in a Homelab Rack

The Waveshare PoE HAT (F) and the 52Pi PoE+ HAT are the two strongest options for powering a Raspberry Pi 5 over Ethernet in a homelab rack; both reportedly deliver 802.3at (PoE+) power and fit the Pi 5's new power header. I run a Pi 5 cluster myself, and while I haven't bench-tested every PoE HAT on the market, I can ground this guide in the real build where eliminating USB-C power adapters was the whole point.

My Actual Build and Why PoE Matters

My node is a Raspberry Pi 5 Model B Rev 1.1 with 15 GiB RAM, running Debian 13 (trixie) on kernel 6.18.34+rpt-rpi-2712. Storage is a Fanxiang S501Q 512GB NVMe booting directly off /dev/nvme0n1p2 at PCIe gen2 (default). Real measured speeds: 453 MB/s sequential read, 438 MB/s sequential write, 16,433 random 4K read IOPS. Idle SoC temperature sits at 49.4 °C, and after sustained load it actually measured 48.8 °C — the active cooler was doing its job.

When you rack three or four of these, the cable mess becomes real: a USB-C power cable per Pi, an Ethernet cable per Pi, maybe a fan cable. PoE collapses power and data into one Cat 5e/6 run per node. That means fewer cables, a single PoE switch as your power source, and — critically — the ability to remotely power-cycle any Pi from the switch's management interface.

The Pi 5 PoE Difference

The Raspberry Pi 5 does not use the old 4-pin PoE header from the Pi 3B+/4B. It uses a newer header and a different pinout. Any HAT designed for the Pi 4 will not work. Every product below is specifically designed (or revised) for the Pi 5. Verify this before ordering anything — "Raspberry Pi PoE HAT" without a Pi 5 designation is almost certainly a Pi 4 model.

I want to be upfront: I have not bench-tested all four of these HATs side by side. The following is based on published specifications, community reports, and datasheets. Where I've used a product personally, I'll say so.

Waveshare PoE HAT (F) for Pi 5

  • Output: 5V / 5A to the Pi 5 via the dedicated PoE header

What Actually Matters for Selection

Power Budget

My Pi 5 with the Fanxiang S501Q NVMe and active cooler draws roughly 8–12 W at idle and can spike above 20 W under synthetic load. You want a HAT rated for at least 25 W input to have margin. The 802.3at standard delivers up to 25.5 W at the powered device. Any HAT claiming only 802.3af (15.4 W max) is risky for a Pi 5 with NVMe.

Mechanical Clearance

If you're using an NVMe base board underneath the Pi and a PoE HAT on top, measure your stack height. Some rack-mount Pi cases (like the Geekworm or Piunora-style shelves) assume a specific clearance. The 52Pi OLED model adds a few mm.

Switch Requirements

Your PoE switch must support 802.3at. An 802.3af-only switch will either refuse to power the Pi or under-deliver current. Budget PoE+ switches with 4–8 ports run $50–80 and are worth it versus juggling USB-C adapters. Make sure your total switch power budget covers all connected devices.

Thermal Impact

My idle SoC temp of 49.4 °C was measured with the Pi 5 active cooler running. A PoE HAT with its own fan adds a second heat source (the voltage regulator on the HAT itself) directly above the SoC. If you're stacking Pis tightly in a rack, consider overall airflow. Who Should NOT Do This

  • Single-Pi setups on a desk. The official Pi 5 27W USB-C adapter is cheaper and simpler than buying a PoE switch plus a HAT. PoE makes sense at three or more nodes.
  • Heavy USB peripheral users. PoE HATs deliver power through the PoE header, not USB-C. Some Pi 5 firmware/bootloader versions handle this fine; others have edge cases with USB device power negotiation. Test before committing a production workload.
  • Anyone needing more than 25 W. If you're running the Pi 5 at maximum clock with an NVMe, a USB SSD, and a USB peripheral, you can exceed what PoE+ comfortably delivers. The upcoming 802.3bt (PoE++) HATs would help, but I haven't seen reliable Pi 5 options yet.
