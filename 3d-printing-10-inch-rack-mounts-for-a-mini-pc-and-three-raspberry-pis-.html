3D-Printing 10-Inch Rack Mounts for a Mini PC and Three Raspberry Pis in PETG

A 10-inch rack is the sweet spot for a homelab that lives on a shelf instead of consuming a closet. I printed two PETG rack mounts — one for a Lenovo M720q Tiny and one holding three Raspberry Pis — on a Bambu Lab P1S, bolted them together with M3/M4 hardware, and slotted them into a 6U Lab Rax frame alongside UniFi networking gear. Here's exactly how, with real numbers from both machines.

What's in the Rack

My 10-inch 6U Lab Rax system holds, top to bottom:

  • UniFi Cloud Gateway Fiber — the router and UniFi controller
  • UniFi USW Flex 2.5G (8-port, all PoE++) — powers everything that takes PoE
  • UniFi U5G — 5G cellular WAN failover
  • Lenovo ThinkCentre M720q Tiny — in a 3D-printed PETG rack mount
  • Three Raspberry Pis (including a Pi 5) — in a 3D-printed PETG triple mount

A UniFi U7 Pro XG access point sits outside the rack, PoE-powered from the same switch.

That's the entire build. Six rack units, one power strip, no full-depth server rails needed.

The 3D-Printed Mounts

Designs and Credit

Both rack mounts — the M720q tray and the triple-Pi shelf — are community designs I downloaded from Bambu Lab MakerWorld. I did not design or model them. I didn't record the specific designer names or model URLs, so search MakerWorld for "10-inch rack M720q" and "10-inch rack Raspberry Pi" and you'll find several options. I also printed a replacement PCIe bracket for the 2.5GbE card inside the M720q, because the low-profile bracket that shipped with the Intel I226-V card didn't fit the M720q's rear opening.

Why PETG

PLA is easier to print but softens around 55–60 °C. My Pi 5 idles at 50.7 °C and hits 54.0 °C under sustained load — that's surface temperature of nearby plastic territory. PETG is commonly rated for continuous use up to roughly 70–80 °C, which gives real margin. It also resists creep better under the constant load of a machine sitting on a shelf for years. The trade-off: PETG is stringier to print and slightly less rigid than PLA. For a rack tray that just needs to hold a 1.3 kg box and sit still, that's a fine deal.

I didn't record my exact print settings, infill percentage, or print times. General guidance: most people print PETG at 230–250 °C hotend, 75–85 °C bed, with 20–30% infill for structural parts. A Bambu Lab P1S in an enclosed chamber handles PETG with almost no fuss — it's the main reason I use that printer for functional parts.

Assembly

Every mount is held together with M3 and M4 nuts and bolts — no heat-set inserts. The MakerWorld designs I used have through-holes sized for bolt-and-nut assembly, which is simpler and more forgiving of slight print dimensional variance. An M3/M4 Metal Screw & Pin Assortment Box – 500 Pieces with Spanners covers both mounts and leaves plenty of spares. You tighten the bolts just enough to clamp the ears to the rack rails — overtighten and you'll crack the PETG at the bolt holes.

The Machines: Real Numbers Side by Side

Both machines were benchmarked with the same tools — fio for storage (1M sequential and 4K random at QD32, direct I/O, 15 seconds per test) and the 7-Zip built-in benchmark for CPU. Same OS base (Debian 13 / trixie).

Metric Raspberry Pi 5 (16 GB) Lenovo M720q (i5-9400T, 32 GB)
Sequential read 452 MB/s 3,424 MB/s (7.6×)
Sequential write 431 MB/s 3,242 MB/s
4K random read (QD32) 104,978 IOPS 158,595 IOPS (1.5×)
7-Zip single-thread 4,463 MIPS 5,473 MIPS (1.2×)

The M720q's Samsung PM9A1 NVMe obliterates the Pi's Fanxiang S501Q on sequential I/O — that's a PCIe Gen 3×4 drive versus Gen 2 behind the Pi's M.2 HAT. Random 4K is closer because that's more about controller firmware than raw bandwidth. Single-thread CPU is only 1.2× apart; the M720q pulls ahead on multi-thread thanks to two extra cores.

The temperature gap matters for a rack. The Pi 5 idles above 50 °C even with PoE-provided power (measured at 5.16 V, no undervoltage or throttle flags). In an enclosed 10-inch rack, that heat has nowhere to go. The M720q's own fan keeps it at 28 °C idle, 39 °C under load — it barely gets warm.

What Each Machine Runs

The Pi 5 runs 13 always-on services: trading bots, a content pipeline, monitoring, and a dashboard. It boots from the Fanxiang S501Q 512 GB NVMe via the official M.2 HAT and draws power solely via PoE from the switch — no wall PSU. That decision came from experience: with both PoE and a wall PSU connected, a breaker trip left the Pi powered off when mains returned — the two sources raced on restore. PoE-only fixed it. Pulling and reinserting the Ethernet cable now cold-boots the Pi by itself.

The M720q runs Proxmox VE 9.2: an LXC Samba file server, an LXC Syncthing instance backing up phone photos (16 GB, 4,181 files), and a nightly pull-backup of the Pi that I've restore-tested. Idle RAM usage is 1.9 GB of 32 GB, so there's headroom for more containers or a full VM. I paid £345 refurbished (October 2026), which included the 32 GB RAM and 1 TB NVMe already installed. I didn't record the Pi build cost, so I can't give an honest price comparison — price a Pi 5 with 16 GB, NVMe HAT, drive, case, and PoE HAT on the day and compare for yourself.

The 2.5GbE upgrade used the Lenovo 01AJ940 riser (the correct part for the M720q/M920q Tiny — not the 01AJ902, which fits the older M710q/M910q) plus an Intel I226-V single-port PCIe card. The card linked at 2,500 Mb/s full duplex to the UniFi USW Flex 2.5G on the first attempt. I haven't measured real throughput beyond that link speed — nothing else on the network can saturate 2.5 GbE yet because the Pi is 1 GbE.

The 5G Backup

The UniFi U5G provides WAN failover to the Cloud Gateway Fiber. It's a 5G RedCap device — published specs list 220 Mbps down / 120 Mbps up on 5G SA, with LTE fallback up to 195 Mbps down. It takes a single nano-SIM or one eSIM (not both simultaneously), has one Gigabit Ethernet port powered by 802.3af PoE, and requires a UniFi gateway on Network 10.4.57+. One US reviewer with a Google Fi SIM and average indoor signal reported roughly 30–85 Mbps down and 1–3 Mbps up. I haven't measured my own speeds, latency, carrier performance, or failover timing — it's there as insurance and I haven't needed to characterise it yet.
