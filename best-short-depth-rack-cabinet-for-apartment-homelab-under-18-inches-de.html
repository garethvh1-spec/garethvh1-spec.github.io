Best Short-Depth Rack Cabinet for Apartment Homelab (Under 18 Inches Deep)

If your homelab lives in a closet, spare bedroom, or behind a couch, a standard 36-inch-deep server rack is absurd overkill. A short-depth rack under 18 inches accommodates single-board computers, patch panels, network switches, and small NAS units while keeping a footprint your landlord (or partner) won't object to.

Why Short-Depth Matters in an Apartment

Standard 4-post racks exist for full-size 1U/2U servers with redundant power supplies. Most apartment homelabs don't run those. They run a Raspberry Pi or two, a mini PC, a managed switch, maybe a UPS, and a patch panel. None of that equipment exceeds 10–12 inches of depth, so a 36-inch rack wastes two feet of space in every direction for no reason.

Short-depth racks (roughly 12–18 inches deep) wall-mount or sit on a desk. They accept standard 19-inch rack-mount gear but stop at the depth where apartment-scale hardware actually ends.

The Four Racks Worth Considering

I haven't personally rack-mounted equipment in all four of these, so I'm basing comparisons on published specs, user reports, and measurements I could verify. Here's an honest breakdown.

NavePoint 9U Wall Mount Rack Enclosure

NavePoint 9U Wall Mount Rack Enclosure

Based on published specs, this enclosure is roughly 15.75 inches deep with a locking glass front door and removable side panels. It's fully enclosed, which matters for dust and noise dampening. Reported usable depth is around 14.5 inches. Nine rack units is enough for a patch panel, a switch, a shelf holding one or two SBCs, and a small UPS. The main trade-off: it's heavier than open frames (~40 lbs empty per the spec sheet), and wall-mounting it into apartment drywall alone is risky — you need studs or a plywood backer board.

StarTech 12U Open Frame Rack

StarTech 12U Open Frame Rack

This is a freestanding open-frame rack, reportedly about 18 inches deep (right at the limit). The 12U height gives you meaningful room to grow. Published specs put the weight capacity around 200 lbs. Open frames provide excellent airflow but zero noise isolation and no dust protection. If your rack lives in a closet with a door, that's fine. If it's in a living room, expect visible cable runs and audible fan noise from anything with active cooling.

Tripp Lite 6U Wall Mount Rack

Tripp Lite 6U Wall Mount Rack

At 6U, this is the smallest option here. Based on specs, the usable depth is around 14.5 inches. Tripp Lite's build quality is generally well-regarded. The limitation is straightforward: six rack units fills up fast. A patch panel, a switch, and a shelf for a Pi leaves you with maybe 2–3U of space. Fine if you know your setup is small and staying small. A poor choice if you're likely to add a second node or a UPS later.

AC Infinity RACK KIYO 9U

AC Infinity RACK KIYO 9U

AC Infinity's KIYO line reportedly includes integrated quiet fans with speed controllers. Published depth is about 15.5 inches. The aesthetic is noticeably cleaner than the NavePoint or Tripp Lite — tempered glass front, matte black finish. Based on user reports, the included fans run around 18–25 dBA, which is genuinely quiet. The trade-off is price: it typically runs $30–80 more than the NavePoint for similar internal volume.

My Actual Build Inside a Short-Depth Setup

My primary node is a Raspberry Pi 5 Model B Rev 1.1 with 15 GB of usable RAM, running Debian GNU/Linux 13 (trixie) on kernel 6.18.34+rpt-rpi-2712. Boot and storage run off a Fanxiang S501Q 512GB NVMe drive connected over the Pi 5's PCIe slot at Gen 2 (the default).

Real measured storage performance:

  • Sequential read: 453 MB/s
  • Sequential write: 438 MB/s
  • Random 4K read IOPS: 16,433

Thermal performance in a rack shelf context matters because enclosed cabinets restrict airflow. My measured SoC temperatures: 49.4°C at idle and 48.8°C after sustained load (the load figure being slightly lower is real — the active cooler spins up under load and overshoots slightly). These numbers were taken at an ambient room temperature around 22°C. Inside a fully enclosed rack without integrated fans, I'd expect idle temps to climb 5–8°C based on general thermal behavior in restricted airflow. An open-frame rack or one with active fans (like the KIYO's built-in fans) would keep temps much closer to my open-air readings.

The entire Pi + NVMe + a small network switch fits comfortably in a 1U shelf with inches to spare. A 9U rack is generous for this kind of setup. Even with a patch panel, PoE switch, UPS, and two Pi nodes, you'd use maybe 6U.

Who Should NOT Buy a Short-Depth Rack

  • Anyone running actual rack servers. A used Dell R720 is 29 inches deep. It physically will not fit. If your plan involves 1U or 2U servers, buy a proper full-depth rack.
  • Anyone who needs more than ~16U. Short-depth wall-mount racks max out around 12–15U. Beyond that you're into full-size territory anyway.
  • Anyone prioritizing density over footprint. Short-depth racks sacrifice volume. That's the entire point, and the entire limitation.

Verdict

For most apartment homelabs, the NavePoint 9U or AC Infinity KIYO 9U hit the sweet spot — enclosed, wall-mountable, and deep enough for SBCs, switches, and a UPS without wasting space. Go KIYO if integrated quiet fans and aesthetics justify the price premium; go NavePoint if you'd rather save $50 and add your own fans later. The StarTech 12U open frame makes sense only if you have a closet to hide it in and want room to grow.
